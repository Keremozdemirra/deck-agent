# Storyline First

Most bad decks are not badly designed. They are decks whose argument was never written
down, so the argument was assembled slide by slide while the slides were being made —
which means it was assembled in whatever order the underlying analysis happened to be
finished in. The symptom is a deck where every slide is defensible and the whole thing
persuades nobody, because there is no sentence anywhere that the audience is being asked
to accept.

This skill forces the argument into a form where its defects are visible before any
effort is spent on layout: a numbered list of complete declarative sentences, read in
order, with nothing else on the page. If the sequence does not persuade as bare prose,
no chart, template or animation will rescue it. The cost of finding that out here is
twenty minutes. The cost of finding it out in the meeting is the decision.

## When to use / when not to

Use it when the deck exists to move someone — approve a capex, change a plan, buy a
thing, accept a finding. Use it when a report or model has to become slides. Use it when
an existing deck is being rebuilt and the complaint is "it doesn't land".

Do not use it for material that is not an argument: a status pack read from a dashboard,
a data appendix, a training walk-through, a document deliberately written as a document.
Those have a structure imposed by their subject and forcing a thesis onto them adds
nothing. Do not use it when the audience has already decided and wants the record — write
the record.

## Preconditions

Do not start until these four are on the page. If the requester cannot supply them, that
is the first finding, and it is usually the real problem.

- **The decision.** What will be different after the meeting? "Inform the board" is not a
  decision; "approve €24m and mandate PPA negotiation" is.
- **The decider.** One named person or body. Not "stakeholders".
- **The counter-position.** What that person currently believes, or what they will do if
  nobody presents. Every storyline is an argument against something.
- **The time.** Minutes on the agenda, not slide count. Slide count is an output of this
  method, not an input.

## The method

1. **Write the governing sentence.** One sentence, stating what the decider should do or
   accept and the single strongest reason. It must contain a subject the decider
   controls, a finite verb, and exactly one *because*. Duarte's formulation of a "big
   idea" is the same instrument: one complete sentence carrying your point of view and
   what is at stake (Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*, Wiley, 2010).

2. **Write the opening as four sentences: situation, complication, question, answer.**
   The situation is something the decider already accepts. The complication is what
   changed to make it a problem now. The question is the one the complication provokes.
   The answer is the governing sentence, verbatim. This is Minto's SCQA introduction
   (Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*, 3rd edition, 1996; Pearson reissue 2009). If
   the situation is contestable, or the complication is not new, the deck has no reason
   to exist yet.

3. **Write the supporting sentences.** Ask the question the governing sentence provokes
   in the decider's head — usually *why should I believe that?* or *how would we do it?*
   — and answer it in complete sentences. Write them fast and long; the tests come next.
   Do not think about slides.

4. **Apply the slide test to every sentence.** Five questions, in this order. A sentence
   must pass all five to become a headline.
   - **Assertion.** Could an informed, hostile reader disagree with it? If nobody can
     disagree, it is a topic label or a truism, not a claim. "Market overview" and "CBAM
     is a challenge for exporters" both fail here.
   - **Singularity.** Does it contain *and*, *while*, a semicolon, or two verbs with
     different subjects? Then it is two sentences. Split it and re-run the test on each
     half. This is the backlog item's rule and it is the one that does the most work.
   - **Evidence.** Name — out loud, now — the one exhibit that would make a sceptic
     accept it. One table, one chart, one quotation, one clause of a regulation. If you
     cannot name it, the sentence is a hope, not a claim.
   - **Necessity.** Delete the sentence. Does the governing sentence still stand? If yes,
     the sentence belongs in the appendix or nowhere.
   - **Length.** Over roughly fourteen words, or three lines at headline size, it is
     almost always two claims wearing one coat. Go back to Singularity.

5. **Check the groups.** Sentences that sit at the same level must be the same *kind* of
   answer — all reasons, or all steps, or all components — never a mix. Together they
   must be sufficient to support the sentence above them, and they must not overlap.
   That is Minto's MECE requirement (*The Pyramid Principle*, 1996) and it is the
   difference between three reasons and three things you happened to analyse.

6. **Order by dependency, not by workstream.** Sentence *n* may only rely on what
   sentences 1…*n*−1 have established. Where two orders are both valid, put first the one
   the decider is most likely to reject — you want the objection surfaced while there is
   still time to answer it, not in the Q&A.

7. **Read the sequence aloud, alone, with no other words.** Governing sentence, then 1 to
   *n*. Every gap where you find yourself adding a connective out loud ("obviously",
   "so then", "the point being") is a missing sentence. Add it and re-test it.

8. **Assign one exhibit per sentence.** The sentence is the message; the exhibit is
   chosen to carry that message, not the reverse. Determine the message first, then the
   comparison it requires, then the chart form (Gene Zelazny, *Say It With Charts*,
   McGraw-Hill, 4th edition, 2001). A sentence whose exhibit is "bullet list of the
   sentence" is a sentence with no evidence — send it back to test 4.

9. **Fix the length last.** Repository convention: budget two minutes per slide for a
   decision meeting with discussion, one minute for a read-out. If the sentence count
   exceeds the budget, cut sentences by re-running test 4 — never by merging two
   sentences onto one slide, which reintroduces exactly the fault this method removes.

## The hard parts

**The sentence that is really two.** The most common failure and the easiest to hide,
because the compound sentence is usually *true*. "Our emissions are high and our costs
are rising" is true and unslideable: the evidence for each half is a different exhibit,
and an audience cannot hold two claims while looking at one picture. Split on the
conjunction, then ask whether both halves survive Necessity. Often one was carrying the
other.

**The sentence nobody can disagree with.** Section dividers, agenda slides, "background",
"our approach", "key takeaways". These are furniture: they organise the author's effort,
not the reader's belief. Delete them. If the decider genuinely needs orientation, that is
one sentence stating what the next three sentences will establish — a claim about the
argument, which is still a claim.

**The sentence you cannot evidence.** Two honest dispositions and one dishonest one. Cut
it, or demote it to a stated assumption on the slide that depends on it. The dishonest
disposition is to keep it as a headline and put a decorative chart underneath; that is
how decks acquire numbers nobody can source. Where a figure is a placeholder, mark it as
illustrative *in the storyline*, not later.

**The sentence that belongs to a different deck.** Real, evidenced, interesting, and
irrelevant to the governing sentence — usually the analysis someone worked hardest on.
Necessity is the only defence. Park it in a named appendix list so the author can see it
survived.

**When the storyline changes the answer.** Sometimes the sequence, read aloud, does not
support the governing sentence — the evidence runs the other way, or step 5 shows a
missing branch nobody analysed. This is the method working, not failing. Stop, say so,
and rewrite the governing sentence. Building the deck anyway produces a document that
argues against itself in front of the decider.

**Bilingual decks.** Write the storyline in the language the deck will be delivered in.
Turkish headlines translated from English sentences drift towards nominalisation
("emisyon azaltımının değerlendirilmesi") and quietly fail the Assertion test — the verb
disappears and the claim goes with it. Keep a finite verb in every Turkish headline.

## Output format

A single markdown file, `storyline.md`, produced before any slide tool is opened.

```markdown
# <deck name> — storyline
Decision: <what changes after the meeting>
Decider: <name or body>   Counter-position: <what they believe now>   Time: <minutes>

## Governing sentence
<one sentence, one because>

## Opening (S-C-Q-A)
S: … | C: … | Q: … | A: <governing sentence, verbatim>

## Sentence sequence
| # | Sentence (= slide headline) | Exhibit | Evidence status |
|---|-----------------------------|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | …                           | …       | sourced / illustrative — needs <source> |

## Rejected sentences
| Sentence | Failed test | Disposition |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| …        | Singularity | split into 4 and 5 |

## Appendix candidates
- …
```

Hand back the headline list on its own as well — numbered sentences, nothing else. That
list is the deck's specification; anyone building slides works from it, and any slide
whose headline is not on it does not get built.

## Sources

- Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking*, 3rd edition,
  1996 (Pearson reissue, 2009) — SCQA introduction; MECE grouping; each idea summarising
  the ideas below it.
- Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*, Wiley, 2010 — the big idea as a single complete sentence
  stating a point of view and what is at stake.
- Gene Zelazny, *Say It With Charts*, McGraw-Hill, 4th edition, 2001 — the message
  determines the comparison, and the comparison determines the chart form.

The slide test (five questions), the dependency-ordering rule, the two-minutes-per-slide
budget and the ban on merging sentences to save length are this repository's own
conventions, not attributed method.
